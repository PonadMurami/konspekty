Meeting Goal + Introduction for the Animator
============================================

After Saturday prayer, the desire to remain with Christ while joyfully awaiting his coming matures in us. We know that in many aspects of life we need conversion.
During this meeting we want to talk about the fact that conversion is not only a turning point in our life, but a process that, consciously conducted, will prepare us for the coming of the Kingdom. We talked about the fact that "going blind" is a process and therefore it is treacherous. Regaining spiritual sight in everyday life also requires time - this is good news! We have infinitely many opportunities for this every day.

Let the thought intertwine through this meeting that we can live similarly as before, but more consciously, that this is exactly what metanoia is - a transformation of thinking taking place gradually, directing our motivation towards the Kingdom: the one that is already among us and the one whose arrival we await.

Meeting Goals:
    - Looking at our life as a way and conversion as a process.
    - Discussing Advent in the Church and the upcoming Advent in a personal context.
    - Introducing the perspective of Christ as King and His Kingdom, considering changes of perspective in faith.

Metanoia as a Way
=================

In this part of the meeting we focus on our understanding of "conversion". We want to realize that every day we have a chance to convert anew and thus get closer to the Kingdom.

Metanoia, Greek "μετανοια", metanoia, 'transformation (meta) of the mind (nous)', Latin equivalent conversio – change of mind, spiritual or intellectual transformation.

    From that time Jesus began to preach, saying, “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.”

    -- Matt 4:17


**Repent**, not have repented! (Note: In Polish "Nawracajcie się" is imperfective/continuous, implying "keep repenting" or "be repenting").

.. note:: Dear Animator, in this place it is worth drawing participants' attention to the imperfective aspect of this verb :)

* What does Jesus call for?

* How can I convert if I am already a believer?

The Greek word "metanoeite", which appears in the calls of John the Baptist, Jesus and the Apostles, assumes a profound change in thinking and feelings, therefore a fundamental transformation, and not a superficial turn. It is not merely an "embellishment" or a change of lifestyle in the sense of pious rituals and traditions.
John the Baptist points out to the Pharisees that baptism is not just about immersion in the river and that belonging to the Chosen People alone is not enough.

Metanoia is therefore a **transformation of the mind**, never-ending work on our fundamental motivation, which every day in a way not always conscious will shape our "small" and "large" life choices.

Conversion is a "transcendence", but not a complete crossing out of what was important so far. It should not be reduced merely to turning away from sin and evil, but raised higher, towards doing greater good.

In the cycle of metanoia, therefore, I do not cut myself off drastically from everything that seems unnecessary to me at a given moment, but I continue the cycle of mind transformation gradually narrowing down so as to "orbit" closer and closer to the center, that is the **Kingdom**.

* What opportunities for conversion do I see on my way (both in the near future and in the broader perspective of my life)?

Advent as Waiting for the King's Return
=======================================

General introduction about Advent and summary of participants' knowledge. The animator tells the history of Advent in their own words or uses prepared information as a form of activation. Beforehand, print the text, cut it into several parts and distribute to participants. Each person can read or tell in their own words the information found on the received card.

Meaning of the word "Advent":
    Advent is a translation of the Greek words: epifaneia and parusia. This term in Rome meant the official arrival of a state dignitary and taking office. However, in religious language it meant the annual arrival of a deity to the temple. In Christianity, the term "adventus" became a synonym for the coming of Christ in the sense of his Incarnation (epifaneia), as well as the second coming in glory (parusia). In the 5th century, to designate the time of preparation for the Lord's birth, we also meet the term: "ante natale Domini" or "adventus Domini".

History of Advent:
    We meet the first traces of celebrating Advent in the 4th century in the Gallic and Spanish liturgy. In a work attributed to Hilary of Poitiers we find a mention of fasting before Christmas. This is the oldest testimony about a special time of preparation for honoring the mystery of the Incarnation. The Synod of Saragossa (389) demanded that the faithful attend church from December 17 until the feast of Epiphany. In Gaul, Bishop Perpetuus of Tours introduced the obligation of fasting on three days of the week (Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays) during the six weeks from St. Martin's Day (November 11) to the Lord's Birth. In Rome, the period of preparation for Christmas was introduced only in the second half of the 6th century. Under the influence of the liturgy of the East, Saturdays were not fasted, so Advent began on December 1 or even November 11 to obtain the number of 40 days of fasting (following the example of Lent). In the 8th and 9th centuries, the preparation period fell on October 30 or even September 24 or 14. Since the time of Gregory the Great (590-604), Advent in Rome included 4 weeks. It was a time of direct, liturgical preparation for celebrating the commemoration of Christ's coming.

History of Advent:
    At the beginning of the 9th century, Advent also acquires an eschatological character - it becomes a time of preparation for the final coming of Christ at the end of time. Liturgical texts of Advent show figures of the Old and New Testaments, through whose life and activity God announced and prepared the world for the coming of Christ. Advent is a time of joyful waiting for the meeting with Jesus. Waiting for the coming of Jesus is a source of joy. Although this preparation consists of waiting and penance, hence the violet color of liturgical vestments, penance is associated with joy. Advent reminds us that our whole life is waiting.

History of Advent:
    In the Polish Church, Advent is enriched with a service called "roraty". Roraty is a votive Mass in honor of the Blessed Virgin Mary, celebrated daily before dawn. They constitute a call for Christians to vigilance in waiting for the coming of the Savior and readiness for the Last Judgment. The name "roraty" comes from the first words of the song accompanying the beginning of the Mass in the period of Advent "Rorate coeli desuper..." (Drop down ye heavens from above). According to historical accounts, roraty date back to the 12th century. This custom was probably brought to Poland by bl. Kinga of Hungary, wife of the Prince of Krakow Bolesław the Chaste. Kinga, as historical sources state, tried to imitate the Blessed Virgin Mary with her life. Such a monument to Kinga's devotion to the Mother of God was the introduction in the Wawel church, and then in the Starosądecki convent (which she founded) of the custom of celebrating roraty.

Symbols of Advent:
    Associated with celebrating the Roraty Mass is the custom of lighting an additional candle, the so-called "roratka". This candle, often decorated with a blue ribbon, reminds us of the figure of Mary, who is an example for us of readiness for the coming of the Messiah. In older times, as historical sources state, before the beginning of the Holy Mass, a custom was practiced that the king approached the altar carrying a decorated candle and placed it on the highest candlestick in the middle of the altar. Representatives of all states brought similar candles to the altar. Approaching the altar and placing candles, they uttered the words "I am ready for God's judgment". Previously, however, they cleansed their consciences and hearts. Today, the symbol of our readiness for the coming of Jesus are four candles lit in subsequent weeks of Advent. The Roraty candle is lit after opening the temple and welcomes those entering with light. The custom of bringing lanterns with you is also practiced. Relatively young, because known in Poland only for over 160 years, is the custom of hanging an Advent wreath in the church during Advent. Four candles are placed on the wreath (symbol of 4 Advent Sundays). Light, greenery and the circle have their meaning (light, hope, life, God). The whole wreath is a symbol of the community waiting in love for the coming of the Lord. On Christmas Eve, 4 burning candles mean the coming of Jesus, who is the light of the world.

Kingdom of God
==============

What does the Kingdom of the King look like? We want to answer this question by comparing two fragments from the Scripture concerning the Kingdom of God.

I assume (maybe wrongly), that it was difficult for us to answer clearly and precisely the question of what the Kingdom really is. Some of us may have completely different ideas about this subject. In our world, there are (and were) various explanations of the Kingdom. We can even take a look at them.

1. In ancient Israel, the Kingdom of God was understood as the absolute rule of God over his people. The period when the twelve tribes of Israel under the leadership of Moses and Joshua wandered in the desert and conquered the land of Canaan was understood as a period of higher social order and the rule of the Lord – an ideal age, golden.

2. The Kingdom of God, the Kingdom of Heaven - a concept concerning the renewal of the world by the announced Messiah in the theology of Judaism and Christianity, including Catholicism, Orthodoxy, Lutheranism, Pentecostalism, which recognize its realization in Jesus Christ and his Church.

3. In Judaism, the Kingdom of God is the world that is to come as the kingdom of God himself, also called the "Kingdom of the Almighty". Jews believe that their duty, mission is already now "perfecting the world through the Kingdom of the Almighty" ("L’takken olam b’malkut Shaddai"). Speaking in this sense about the Kingdom assumes that all actions of Jews consisting in repairing, perfecting the world (e.g. ethical conduct towards each other) take place in reference to God's power over the world and His help.

4. It means reigning, ruling, God's power in the world he created, and especially in the microcosm - in the small world, that is in man. Thanks to this rule, God realized the ideal of a just King; this rule consisted in caring for the poor, weak and needy. This rule of God revealed itself in a special way in Jesus Christ as a living, dynamic and saving reality. In the person of Jesus, in His words and deeds, God's rule was revealed along with all saving powers. The early Christian writer Origen called Jesus the Kingdom of God.

5. The final revelation of God at the end of time has not yet occurred. Christians therefore live, using theological nomenclature, in eschatological tension between "already" and "not yet" of the fullness of God's kingdom.

It is like leaven that a woman took and hid in three measures of flour, until it was all leavened.”
    - "Again, the kingdom of heaven is like a net that was thrown into the sea and gathered fish of every kind."
    - "The kingdom of heaven may be compared to a king who gave a wedding feast for his son."

The ideal summary of the topic of the Kingdom let be the words of Jesus:

    Jesus answered, “My kingdom is not of this world. If my kingdom were of this world, my servants would have been fighting, that I might not be delivered over to the Jews. But my kingdom is not from the world.” Then Pilate said to him, “So you are a king?” Jesus answered, “You say that I am a king. For this purpose I was born and for this purpose I have come into the world—to bear witness to the truth. Everyone who is of the truth listens to my voice.”

    -- John 18:36-37

In Jesus' understanding, ruling is serving. Jesus' kingdom is not of this world, because in it, it is not the subjects who fight for their king, but it is the king who fights and sacrifices himself for them. He serves them. Such is God's understanding of reigning, different from the generally accepted one. God never wanted to be a ruler in the human sense of the word. He didn't even want the Israelites to have a king. It was their invention, not His will. He knew that we as humans have a distorted image of power and abuse it too often.

The Scripture itself does not explain the concept of the Kingdom of God to us in a concrete way, leaving us with a certain dose of mystery. And maybe this mystery is the point. Never, in our earthly life, will we be given full knowledge of the mystery of the Kingdom, because it has not yet fully revealed itself. Part of the mystery will remain in it, which is an integral element of our faith. But this does not mean that we cannot strive for it and participate in its realization on earth. Each of us, at least to some extent, can intuitively discern the direction of the way to the Kingdom. It is worth investing in this way.
